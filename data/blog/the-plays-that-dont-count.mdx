Mississippi State's offense averaged 0.069 expected points added per play in 2025.

Or 0.052, depending on whether you throw out eight and a half percent of their snaps.

That is a quarter of a team's headline number, moved by a filter most people never see mentioned. It is worth understanding what that filter does and why it exists.

## Blowouts stop being football

Late in a decided game, both teams stop playing the sport they were playing an hour earlier.

The team ahead runs the ball into the middle of the line, keeps the clock moving, and gets its starters out. The team behind throws underneath against a defense playing fifteen yards off, taking whatever is available because the only currency left is yards and the clock.

Both are behaving rationally. Neither is doing the thing you were measuring.

The trailing offense, in particular, tends to look good. Soft coverage, backup defenders, no pressure. Those drives produce completions and yards and respectable EPA — and they tell you nothing about whether that offense can function when someone is trying.

This has a direction to it. The teams accumulating the most of these snaps are the teams getting blown out, which means **the filter you skip is the one that flatters bad teams.**

## The standard cutoffs

The convention comes from Bill Connelly, and it is the one most public college football work uses. A play is garbage time when the score margin exceeds:

| Quarter       | Margin |
| ------------- | ------ |
| 1st           | 43     |
| 2nd           | 37     |
| 3rd           | 27     |
| 4th and later | 22     |

The thresholds tighten as the game runs down, which is the part that makes obvious sense. A 30-point deficit in the first quarter is a bad afternoon. The same deficit with eight minutes left is over. Time remaining is what makes a margin recoverable, so any sensible rule has to fall as the clock does.

Two mechanical details matter more than they sound like they do.

**The margin is measured before each snap, not for the game.** This is a play-level flag, not a game-level one. The score before the play is what gets checked against the threshold, so a game can move in and out of garbage time as the score changes. A blowout that tightens stops being garbage time the moment the margin comes back under the line.

**It is the margin, not the leader.** The filter removes plays from both offenses. The team ahead is running clock and the team behind is padding, and neither is telling you much.

Across all of FBS in 2025, this flags **10.5% of scrimmage plays.** One snap in ten happens in a game that has already been decided.

## Week 0 provided a demonstration

The 2026 season was eight games old before it produced a clean example.

USC led San Jose State 35-3 through three quarters. The final was 42-26. San Jose State scored 23 points in the fourth quarter against a defense that had allowed three in the first three.

That 32-point margin entering the fourth is ten points past the threshold, so the quarter opens flagged. And you can see why the rule exists by reading the two versions of the game:

|                        | USC | San Jose State |
| ---------------------- | --- | -------------- |
| Through three quarters | 35  | 3              |
| Final                  | 42  | 26             |

The box score records a 16-point game. Anyone scanning a scoreboard the next morning sees a two-score loss for a Mountain West team on the road against a ranked Big Ten opponent, which sounds like a respectable afternoon.

The game was 35-3.

Every one of those fourth-quarter points is real in the sense that it happened. None of it is evidence about how San Jose State's offense functions against USC's defense playing football. Leave those snaps in the season totals and San Jose State's offensive rating goes up, USC's defensive rating goes down, and both numbers are worse descriptions of the two teams than they were before.

One honest complication: as San Jose State scored, the margin shrank. Once it drops to 22 or below, plays stop being flagged and count normally again. So the fourth quarter is not uniformly garbage — the filter turns off partway through, exactly as it should. That is what the play-level check buys you over a cruder rule.

## What it does to Mississippi State

|                                     | EPA per play |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------ |
| All plays                           | 0.069        |
| Garbage time removed                | **0.052**    |
| Garbage removed, FBS opponents only | **0.026**    |

Each filter takes the number down. The first removes snaps taken in decided games. The second removes the Alcorn State game, where a Power Four roster played an FCS one.

The last number is the honest one. Against opponents who could plausibly beat them, in situations where both teams were still competing, MSU's offense was slightly above break-even. That is a much duller claim than 0.069, and it is much more likely to be true.

## The part I did not want to take on faith

I used those four thresholds because everyone does. That is a bad reason to use anything, so I checked them.

The play-by-play data carries a win probability estimate for the situation before each snap. If garbage time means _the outcome is no longer in doubt_, then the thresholds should land where the leading team's win probability is effectively 100%. That is a testable claim.

When I first published this post, I ran that test on 2025 and it came back clean. Every threshold sat above 99% win probability. The first-quarter cutoff came in at 99.95%, on a bucket of 144 plays that I flagged as thin and moved past.

The test was broken, and the way it was broken is the most useful thing in this post.

## The number the filter reads

The garbage-time rule looks at one number per play: the score margin before the snap. The data supplies that margin as a ready-made column. I checked the thresholds. I never checked the column.

The column is wrong. On a play where nobody scores, the margin cannot change, so the column should match the scoreboard. In 2023 and 2024 it does, more than 99% of the time. In 2025 it disagrees on 8.5% of those plays.

Here is what that looks like. Mississippi State against Georgia, third quarter, MSU down 38–7, the first four snaps of a drive:

| Snap | Scoreboard | Margin column | MSU win probability |
| ---- | ---------- | ------------- | ------------------- |
| 1st  | 7–38       | −31           | 0.1%                |
| 2nd  | 7–38       | −31           | 0.1%                |
| 3rd  | 7–38       | **0**         | **58%**             |
| 4th  | 7–38       | −31           | 0.1%                |

For one incomplete pass, the data believed the game was tied.

The margin being wrong is bad. The win probability being wrong along with it is what sank my check. In this feed, win probability moves with the margin column, not with the scoreboard: on the 2025 plays where the two disagree, win probability correlates 0.88 with the column and 0.24 with the actual score. It is computed from the same broken number.

So I had graded the thresholds against a second opinion that had copied the first one's homework. Wherever the margin was wrong, the win probability agreed with it, and the test came back clean because it could not do anything else. **A check built on the same inputs as the thing it checks can only agree with it.**

The first-quarter bucket is where that shows. I called those 144 plays thin and noisy. They were fictional. Rebuilt from the scoreboard, their real margins ran from 0 to 23 points — a median of 7 — scattered across 42 different games. Not one snap of the 2025 season was played in the first quarter with a 43-point margin. The filter had been throwing those snaps away as garbage time, and the win probability, reading the same bad margin, called every one of them decided.

## Rebuilding the margin

The fix is to stop trusting the column. The running score on each play is reliable — it reproduces every game's scoring exactly — so the margin before a snap can be rebuilt from the score after the play before it.

Across 2025 that changes the status of about 1,070 snaps. Roughly 180 competitive snaps had been thrown out, including every first-quarter snap the filter removed all season. Almost 900 snaps from decided games had been kept. The share of plays flagged goes from 10.2% to 10.5%.

For Mississippi State it was three snaps: the Georgia incompletion above, and two in the fourth quarter against Missouri, down 49–24, where the column read −7. All three were garbage time that the filter let through. Taking them out is why the table above reads 0.052 and 0.026.

## The check, run properly

The test itself was a good idea. It just needs inputs it does not share with the thing it is testing. From 2020 through 2024 the margin column is clean — it matches the scoreboard more than 98% of the time — so the win probability built on it is clean too. Here are those five seasons, with the margin rebuilt from the scoreboard:

| Quarter | Margin    | Plays  | Leader's win probability |
| ------- | --------- | ------ | ------------------------ |
| 1st     | 21–27     | 1,815  | 97.0%                    |
| 1st     | 28–34     | 301    | 99.2%                    |
| 1st     | 35–42     | 34     | 98.5%                    |
| 1st     | **43+**   | **2**  | —                        |
| 4th     | 14–20     | 35,014 | 97.6%                    |
| 4th     | **21–27** | 24,461 | **99.5%**                |
| 4th     | 28–34     | 16,727 | 99.9%                    |

Two plays. In five seasons.

Two things come out of this, and only one of them is what I said the first time.

**The thresholds are safe.** Past the fourth-quarter cutoff the leader wins 99.5% of the time, and the second- and third-quarter cutoffs land at about the same level. Nothing in the standard definition is throwing away football that was still competitive — as long as the margin it reads is real.

**The first-quarter cutoff never fires.** A 43-point lead inside the first fifteen minutes is something the data almost never contains, so the rule can't be tested and does next to nothing. I originally wrote that it corresponded to 99.95% certainty; that number was measured on plays that did not exist. If you wanted one uniform rule — say, exclude everything above 99% win probability — the first-quarter threshold would sit somewhere near 28 points, not 43. That conclusion survives. The evidence for it is just different now.

So the numbers are reasonable, but they are a convention rather than a derivation. Nobody solved for them. They were chosen because they work, and they have stuck because they work.

## Why I am keeping them anyway

**Comparability.** If I invent my own cutoffs, my EPA numbers stop lining up with SP+, with other public work, with anything a reader could check me against. Being slightly non-optimal costs less than being incomparable.

**The error runs the right direction.** These thresholds exclude _less_ than a strict win-probability rule would. If I am going to be wrong, I would rather be wrong toward keeping data than toward throwing it away.

**Win probability is not the same thing as garbage time.** This is the part that actually matters. The reason to filter is that both teams change how they play. Win probability is a proxy for that, not a measurement of it. A team up 25 in the fourth quarter is already running clock. A team up 25 in the first is still playing normal football. The score is identical and the behavior is not, which is exactly why the thresholds differ by quarter — and it is why a flat win-probability rule would be worse rather than better.

## What this leaves

Two numbers I trust more than I did: MSU's 2025 offense at 0.026 EPA per play against FBS competition in competitive situations, and a filter I have now argued with twice.

The first argument was with the rule, and the rule held up. The second was with the number the rule reads, and that is where the problem was. I would not have found it by thinking harder about thresholds. It turned up when I read Mississippi State's 2026 opener play by play and saw the data report a 25-point margin on the opening snap, with the score 0–0. Checking a rule is not the same as checking what you feed it.

What it does not fix is the schedule. Those FBS opponents included Georgia and Texas A&M, and they also included Northern Illinois. Averaging across them as though they were the same test is the largest remaining problem with every number on this site.

That one needs a model, not a filter. It is the thing I am building toward.
