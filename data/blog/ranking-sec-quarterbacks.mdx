Everyone ranks quarterbacks. Almost nobody shows how much of the ranking is noise.

Here is a list of every SEC quarterback with 100 or more dropbacks this season, ordered by expected points added per play, adjusted for the defenses he played. And here is the same list with an error bar on every name.

The top three are **Gunner Stockton (+0.273), Keelon Russell (+0.260) and Kamario Taylor (+0.256)**. They are separated by seventeen thousandths of a point. Their error bars are roughly 0.13 to 0.17 wide. Those three names are, statistically, the same name.

That is the first thing worth saying about any quarterback ranking: **the order is mostly arbitrary in the middle, and the only real information is at the ends.** Stockton is clearly better than Marcel Reed. Stockton versus Russell is a coin flip dressed as an opinion.

## What a play is, and what the adjustment does

Two definitions before the rest of it.

**A quarterback's plays** are his dropbacks — pass attempts plus sacks, because a sack is a failed pass play and belongs on his ledger — plus his own carries. The data cannot separate a designed quarterback run from a scramble (the word "scramble" appears zero times in 52,000 rushes), so both count as carries.

**The opponent adjustment is crude, and "crude" is doing real work in that sentence.** Here is exactly what it does:

1. For every FBS defense, compute the average expected points per play it has allowed all season. A defense at **+0.10** gives up a tenth of a point more per snap than a league-average defense. A defense at **−0.10** gives up that much less.
2. For each quarterback, average that number across every play he ran — so the defenses he faced most often count most.
3. Subtract it from his raw figure.

A quarterback who spent the year against defenses averaging +0.10 gets a tenth of a point taken off every play. One who faced −0.03 gets a little added back.

**This is circular and I want to be upfront about it.** Those defensive ratings include the games against these very quarterbacks. When Stockton carves up a defense, he makes that defense's season rating worse, and the adjustment then charges him for having played a bad defense. A proper version solves this iteratively, re-rating offenses and defenses against each other until the numbers stop moving. This one does not. It is one pass, not convergence.

So treat it as a first-order correction that is better than nothing and worse than a real model. It moves people a few places. It does not manufacture anyone.

## Counting the running

<Image
  src="/static/images/qb-2026-pass-vs-rush.png"

  width={1433}

Leaving carries out of a quarterback ranking is a choice, not a neutral default, and it misrepresents at least four of these players.

**Bryce Brown (Auburn)** has 93 carries — more than any quarterback in the league. As a passer he is **−0.044 a dropback**, twelfth of sixteen. As a runner he is **+0.306 a carry**. Count only the throwing and he is one of the worst starters in the SEC; count everything and he is ninth, which is about right.

**LaNorris Sellers (South Carolina)** is the same story with better numbers: +0.157 passing, **+0.525** running on 59 carries.

The reverse case is **Alex Philo (Florida)**, who is the **best pure passer in the conference at +0.314 a dropback** and adds almost nothing with his legs (+0.112). He has the highest passing figure here and finishes sixth overall. Both of those facts are true and the second one is the more useful.

Mississippi State's Taylor sits in the top-right corner with Stockton and Russell: above average at both, which is the whole argument for him.

One honest caveat on that chart: rushing EPA on a small number of carries is noisy. Simmons has 10 carries and MacIntyre has 5, so their vertical positions should be read as "barely runs" rather than as a measurement.

## Where passer rating gets it wrong

<Image
  src="/static/images/qb-2026-rating-vs-epa.png"

Minchey of Kentucky is well right of the line, with a high rating but below-average EPA. T. Chambliss of Ole Miss is left of it, with a low rating and above-average EPA."
  width={1492}
  height={1179}

The old number is better than its reputation. NCAA passer rating correlates with adjusted EPA per play at **0.85**. If you only have the back of a media guide, it will not badly mislead you.

The disagreements are worth naming, because they are not random:

- **Kyle Minchey (Kentucky)** has the sixth-best passer rating in the conference (142.7) and the eleventh-best adjusted EPA (−0.036). He completes passes that do not move the chains.
- **Trinidad Chambliss (Ole Miss)** is the mirror image — a 133.4 rating, eleventh, but +0.163 EPA, seventh.
- **Brown** sits far left of the line, because passer rating has no idea he exists as a runner.

Passer rating counts yards, touchdowns, completions and interceptions. It has no concept of down, distance, field position or situation, and no concept of a quarterback's legs. Where it disagrees with EPA, it is usually one of those blind spots doing the talking.

## What I would not use

Three things I tested and left out, because a ranking built on them would be measuring the scorekeeper.

**Hurries.** 18.4% of team-games in this data record zero quarterback hurries. Pass breakups, by comparison, are missing in only 6.5%. A stat absent from a fifth of games is a charting artifact.

**Average depth of target.** Coverage ranges from 74% of pass plays (Oklahoma) to 96% (Mississippi State), and the gaps are not random: incompletions are thrown far deeper than completions (12.5 yards against 6.2) and are _less_ likely to be recorded (84.8% against 93.5%). Every quarterback's measured throw depth runs short, by an amount that depends on how well his games were charted. It is fine for description and useless for comparison.

**Quarterback fumbles.** 56% of 2026 lost-fumble plays are flagged in this data as non-scrimmage plays, so they vanish from any play-filtered box score. Turnover counts built the obvious way are wrong.

## One more thing about Mississippi State

Taylor is third in this conference by the best measure I can build, and Mississippi State is 4-3.

He has also played five games. He missed the LSU game entirely and lasted one quarter against Oklahoma, which is why he has 170 dropbacks against a list median near 205, and his error bar is wider than the raw number suggests it should be. The thing that makes his season good and the thing that makes it uncertain are the same thing.

---

_How these numbers are built: 2026 scrimmage plays, FBS opponents both ways, garbage time removed, kneels and spikes excluded. A quarterback's plays are his dropbacks (sacks included) and his own carries; passers are identified from the play description, which carries them for 99.3% of SEC pass plays, in either of the two text formats this data uses. Minimum 100 dropbacks, which gives sixteen quarterbacks and excludes backups and platoon splits. EPA is filtered to plays whose underlying expected-points values survive checking. The opponent adjustment subtracts the average EPA per play allowed by the defenses faced, weighted by how many plays were run against each; defenses need 150 plays to be rated. **It is one pass, not an iterative solve, and it is circular** because those defensive ratings include these games. Error bars are one standard error, computed from how much a quarterback's per-game EPA moves week to week, divided by the square root of his games; games with fewer than ten plays are excluded from that calculation. The point estimate is pooled across all plays, so it is not identical to the average of the per-game figures. **No part of this is adjusted for the quality of a quarterback's own receivers, line or play-caller**, which is the largest thing missing. Data from cfbfastR, current through week 8._
