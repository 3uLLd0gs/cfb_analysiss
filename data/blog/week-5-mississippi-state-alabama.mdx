Mississippi State's first drive of the game went eight yards, one yard, four yards, nineteen yards — and then a strip sack at the Alabama 43 that Alabama recovered. Four plays into the season's biggest home game the Bulldogs had moved the ball well and come away with nothing.

The one-yard gain was Fluff Bothwell's. It was the only carry he would take. He left with an ankle injury carried over from the Missouri game, and spent the rest of the afternoon on the sideline in a walking boot.

Alabama won 56-23. Mississippi State falls to 4-1.

Alabama had eleven possessions and scored touchdowns on seven of them. Those seven drives covered 42, 80, 88, 85, 21, 74 and 57 yards. Four of them started at or inside their own 25 and ended in the end zone anyway.

Mississippi State had twelve possessions: three touchdowns, one field goal, four punts, four turnovers.

There was no collapse in this game, which is the strange part. Mississippi State never fell apart in a single catastrophic stretch. Alabama simply scored two touchdowns in every quarter, from the first snap to the last, at exactly the same rate for sixty minutes.

If anything Alabama got **better** after halftime: 55.6% success rate before the break, 66.7% after.

Alabama ran **thirteen fewer plays and gained 166 more yards.**

The obvious objection to all of this is garbage time — a 33-point margin inflates everything. It doesn't hold. Strip the garbage-time snaps out and Alabama still averaged 8.49 yards a play at a 57.4% success rate, against 4.86 and 35.6%. This was the game throughout, not a late pile-on.

## Alabama was never behind schedule

<Image
  src="/static/images/mississippi-state-vs-alabama-downtype.png"
  alt="Bar chart of success rate on standard downs and passing downs. On standard downs Mississippi State was at 41 percent and Alabama 63 percent. On passing downs Mississippi State fell to 21 percent while Alabama rose to 55 percent."
  width={1298}
  height={873}
/>

Split every snap into standard downs — first down, second-and-7 or less, third or fourth and 4 or less — and passing downs, where the defense knows you have to throw.

Mississippi State fell from 41% to 21% once it got behind schedule, which is normal. That is what passing downs are for.

Alabama went the other way. On its eleven passing downs it produced a **55% success rate, 13.6 yards a play, and +1.37 expected points added per snap.** It converted **all three** of its third-and-7-or-longer attempts. The down that is supposed to be the defense's reward was Alabama's best down of the game.

Alabama only faced nine third downs all afternoon. It never faced a third-and-short, because it almost never needed one.

Keelon Russell threw 16 of 22 for 285 yards and three touchdowns without an interception, and he did it at an **average depth of target of 14.0 yards.** His season figure coming in was 9.7. Alabama did not dink its way down the field; it threw over the top of a defense that could not stop the run either.

L. Brooks was targeted an average of **24 yards past the line of scrimmage** and caught four of five for 96 yards. R. Scott drew seven targets at 15.0 yards and scored twice.

Mississippi State allowed seven plays of 20+ yards. It had three.

## The run game, before and after

  src="/static/images/mississippi-state-vs-alabama-rush-by-week.png"

  width={1380}

Without Bothwell, Mississippi State ran for **1.92 yards a carry.** K. Wilson got 10 carries for 27 yards; X. Gayten got 10 for 10.

It would be easy to stop there and call it an injury story. The chart says something more uncomfortable. **Mississippi State's rushing has gotten worse every single week of this season** — 9.72, 6.21, 6.00, 4.28, 2.45 — and Bothwell carried the ball in four of those five weeks. Losing him made a bad trend worse; it did not create it.

Some of that decline is opponent quality, and the early number is inflated by UL Monroe. But the line only points one direction.

## The defense never won a run

Here is the most damning number of the afternoon, and it has nothing to do with who was injured.

|                                    | Mississippi State | Alabama |
| ---------------------------------- | ----------------- | ------- |
| Runs faced                         | 29                | 25      |
| Runs stopped at or behind the line | **0**             | 8       |
| Stuff rate                         | **0%**            | 32%     |
| Sacks                              | 4                 | 4       |
| Quarterback hurries                | 6                 | 5       |
| Passes broken up                   | 1                 | 4       |
| Success rate allowed               | 61.4%             | 34.3%   |

**Mississippi State did not stop a single Alabama run at or behind the line of scrimmage.** Not one, in twenty-nine attempts. Alabama averaged 6.69 yards a carry. T. Brown took 14 carries for 66 yards and two scores; E. Crowell needed three carries to gain 49.

The pass rush was not the problem — four sacks and six hurries is respectable work. The front simply never won at the point of attack.

## Four turnovers, and one that mattered most

Mississippi State gave the ball away four times: a strip sack on the opening drive, an interception returned for a touchdown in the first quarter, a fumble after a completion to S. Magee in the second, and a second interception in the third.

The pick-six is the one that changes a game's shape. Alabama's 56 points came from **eight touchdowns — seven on offense and one by its defense.** Every extra point was good. Mississippi State's 23 came from three touchdowns, a missed extra point, and a field goal.

Alabama turned it over once, a D. Hill fumble in the second quarter. It was one of only two possessions all day that Alabama ended without either punting or scoring; the other was the one the clock ran out on.

## What Mississippi State actually did well

Two things, and they are worth naming.

**Anthony Evans III was excellent.** Fourteen targets, twelve catches, 166 yards and two touchdowns, including a 45-yard strike in the third quarter. With Bothwell gone the offense funneled through him — he saw 14 of Mississippi State's 41 targets — and he still produced.

**When the Bulldogs succeeded, they succeeded big.** Their IsoPPP — average expected points added on successful plays only — was **1.58 against Alabama's 1.17.** Mississippi State's good plays were better than Alabama's good plays. There were just far fewer of them: a 34% success rate against 61%.

That is the whole game in one line. Alabama was relentlessly ordinary-good on sixty-one percent of its snaps. Mississippi State was spectacular on a third of them and nowhere on the rest.

## About that preview

Six days ago I wrote that these two teams were near-twins on a per-play basis — Alabama first in the SEC in offensive EPA, Mississippi State fourth, a tenth of a point between them. The post said explicitly that it was not a prediction. It was still wrong about what mattered.

What it missed was not available in four games of per-play averages: that Mississippi State's margin for error depended on one running back, that its defensive front had not yet faced a line like Alabama's, and that two offenses with similar efficiency numbers can be separated entirely by what happens on passing downs.

The preview did get one thing right, and it is small comfort: it flagged Alabama's third-and-long defense as the sharpest edge in the matchup. It turned out the more important number was Alabama's third-and-long **offense**, which went three for three.
