Mississippi State hosts Missouri on Saturday. Both teams are 3-0. Neither has played anyone the other has played.

This is a description of what each team has done so far, not a prediction of what will happen. Two things make prediction a bad idea here. **Missouri has two games that count** — the FCS opener is excluded, as always — which is 114 offensive snaps. And the opponents are not comparable: Missouri has beaten Kansas 38-21 and Troy 27-17, while Mississippi State has played UL Monroe, Minnesota and South Carolina.

With that said, the numbers are not subtle.

## Missouri is a defense with an offense attached

| Missouri           | Offense       | Defense           |
| ------------------ | ------------- | ----------------- |
| Success rate       | 35%           | **33% allowed**   |
| Yards per play     | 6.1           | **4.2 allowed**   |
| EPA per play       | +0.11         | **−0.12 allowed** |
| Third downs        | 9 of 27 (33%) | 10 of 31 (32%)    |
| Rushing success    | **29%**       | 35% allowed       |
| Plays of 20+ yards | 9             | 6 allowed         |

The defense is legitimately good. The offense has gone backwards every week:

| Week | Opponent                  | Success rate | EPA per play |
| ---- | ------------------------- | ------------ | ------------ |
| 1    | Arkansas-Pine Bluff (FCS) | 59%          | +0.29        |
| 2    | at Kansas                 | 37%          | +0.19        |
| 3    | Troy                      | **33%**      | **−0.02**    |

Against Troy, Missouri ran 46 non-garbage snaps and averaged 5.0 yards on them.

But "bad" is the wrong word for it. Missouri is **volatile**. Measure explosiveness the way Bill Connelly does — average EPA on successful plays only, ignoring the failures — and Missouri comes out _ahead_:

| Explosiveness (EPA on successful plays) | Value    | Successful plays |
| --------------------------------------- | -------- | ---------------- |
| Missouri offense                        | **1.51** | 40               |
| Mississippi State offense               | 1.31     | 79               |

When Missouri's offense works, it works bigger than Mississippi State's. It just works far less often — 35% of snaps against 56%. That is a boom-bust offense, and boom-bust offenses are the kind that can hang around in a game they are being outplayed in.

## Drive quality

Play-level rates understate the gap, because Missouri's failures cluster into dead drives.

|                                | Mississippi State  | Missouri      |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------ | ------------- |
| Drives                         | 27                 | 26            |
| Available yards gained         | **66%**            | 44%           |
| Drives gaining 40+ yards       | **17 of 27 (63%)** | 7 of 26 (27%) |
| Drives of 10+ plays            | 5                  | 1             |
| Trips inside the opponent's 40 | **19**             | 13            |
| Touchdowns on those trips      | **14 of 19 (74%)** | 7 of 13 (54%) |
| Three-and-outs                 | 3                  | 5             |

"Available yards" is the share of the distance in front of you that you actually cover: start on your own 25 and you have 75 yards available. Mississippi State has taken two thirds of what was available; Missouri fewer than half. Missouri has had exactly one drive of ten or more plays this season against FBS opponents.

When Missouri has the ball, its offense and Mississippi State's defense have produced almost identical numbers to date — 35% success against 37% allowed, half of drives reaching the opponent's 40 against 48% allowed. On the other side, Mississippi State's offense has been better than what Missouri's defense has faced by 23 points of success rate.

That is the whole story of the comparison, with the caveat attached: those Missouri defensive numbers were earned against Kansas and Troy, and Mississippi State's offensive numbers include UL Monroe.

## Third downs are the clearest split

Both defenses have the same shape: nearly impossible to convert against on long third downs, very beatable on short ones.

**Mississippi State has allowed 0 of 19 on third-and-7 or longer.** Not one conversion in three games. The only apparent exception was a Minnesota catch originally ruled a first down and overturned on review to a yard short.

Missouri has allowed 2 of 15 in the same situation. But on third-and-3 or less, Missouri has allowed 5 of 8 and Mississippi State 6 of 10.

So the leverage on both sides is second down. An offense that reaches third-and-short converts against either of these defenses; an offense that reaches third-and-long against either one is usually finished.

That pattern is not just a third-down quirk, and the wider version has a much bigger sample behind it. Split every snap into **standard downs** (first down; second-and-7 or less; third or fourth and 4 or less) and **passing downs** (everything else, where the defense knows you have to throw):

| Success rate                          | Standard downs | Passing downs |
| ------------------------------------- | -------------- | ------------- |
| Mississippi State offense             | 59% (118)      | 48% (42)      |
| Missouri offense                      | 41% (70)       | 25% (44)      |
| **Mississippi State defense allowed** | 46% (117)      | **16% (49)**  |
| **Missouri defense allowed**          | 37% (83)       | 25% (52)      |

Sample sizes in parentheses. Mississippi State's defense has been ordinary on standard downs and close to unplayable on passing downs — a 16% success rate allowed across 49 snaps, which is the same story the 0-for-19 tells but on almost three times the evidence.

Missouri's offense falls from 41% to 25% once it is behind schedule. Its problem is not only that passing downs are hard; it is that it reaches them so often: **39% of Missouri's snaps have been passing downs, against 26% of Mississippi State's.**

## Where Mississippi State has been strongest, Missouri has been strongest

Two tendencies that run directly into each other.

**Mississippi State runs up the middle.** 69% of its carries, at 6.2 yards each and a 57% success rate. That is precisely where Missouri's defense is best: 58% of the runs it has faced went up the middle, and it held them to 3.3 yards and a 29% success rate.

| Mississippi State runs | Carries | Yards/carry | Success | Runs of 10+ |
| ---------------------- | ------- | ----------- | ------- | ----------- |
| Left                   | 17      | **10.2**    | **71%** | 7           |
| Middle                 | 53      | 6.2         | 57%     | 5           |
| Right                  | 7       | 5.1         | 57%     | 1           |

| Missouri run defense | Carries faced | Yards/carry | Success allowed |
| -------------------- | ------------- | ----------- | --------------- |
| Left                 | 11            | **4.8**     | **55%**         |
| Middle               | 38            | 3.3         | 29%             |
| Right                | 16            | 3.9         | 38%             |

Mississippi State's left-side runs have produced 10.2 yards a carry and seven gains of 10+ on 17 attempts. Missouri has faced only 11 runs to that side. Both samples are too small to be a plan, but they point the same direction.

**Missouri's defense is best against the short pass**, which is Mississippi State's staple. 83% of Mississippi State's attempts have been classified short, and on those the Bulldogs have completed 73% at 8.5 yards an attempt. Missouri has allowed a 35% success rate on short passes, its strongest coverage number.

| Passing, by depth | MSU offense                         | Missouri defense allowed               |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Short             | 73% comp, 8.5 yds/att, 56% success  | 56% comp, 5.3 yds/att, **35% success** |
| Deep              | 54% comp, 15.9 yds/att, 54% success | 38% comp, 13.4 yds/att, 38% success    |

Deep attempts are 13 and 8 respectively. That is not a sample, it is an anecdote, and it is in the table only because leaving it out would hide how thin the evidence is.

## What to watch

Three things that would tell you early which way this is going, all of them observable in the first quarter:

1. **Whether Mississippi State's run game works away from the middle.** It has been explosive to the left and Missouri has seen almost nothing there.
2. **Whether Missouri's offense gets to second-and-medium.** It has been stuck in third-and-long — 15 of its 27 third downs have been 7 or more yards, and it converted four of them.
3. **Third-and-short.** Both defenses give it up. Whoever spends more snaps there scores more.

---

_How these numbers are built: scrimmage plays only, kneels and spikes removed, FBS opponents only, garbage time removed. Missouri's FCS opener is excluded from every figure except the week-by-week table, where it is labelled. Success rate: a play succeeds if it gains 50% of the needed yards on first down, 70% on second, or 100% on third or fourth; turnovers always fail. Standard downs are first down, second-and-7 or less, and third or fourth and 4 or less; passing downs are everything else. Explosiveness (IsoPPP) is average EPA on successful plays only. Available yards is total yards gained divided by total yards available, where a drive starting on your own 25 has 75 available. Yards per play excludes turnover return yardage, which the raw data credits to the offense. Air yards are not in this data — the "short" and "deep" classifications come from the play-by-play's own description, which covers 90% of pass attempts. Rush direction covers 99% of carries. Data from cfbfastR._
