Mississippi State goes to Baton Rouge on Saturday at 4-1, coming off a bye and a 33-point loss to Alabama. LSU is 4-2, coming off a one-point loss at Kentucky.

On paper this is a clean matchup of opposites: each team is strong exactly where the other is weak. The complication is that Mississippi State's quarterback and its lead running back are both listed as questionable, and nothing below prices that.

This is a description of what both teams have done through six weeks, not a forecast.

Mississippi State is one of four SEC offenses above +0.25 a play. LSU has the only defense in the conference below −0.2. Neither team is balanced, and they are unbalanced in opposite directions.

A caveat that matters more than usual here: **these two have no common FBS opponents.** Mississippi State has played UL Monroe, Minnesota, South Carolina, Missouri and Alabama. LSU has played Clemson, Louisiana Tech, Ole Miss, Texas A&M and Kentucky. There is no opponent adjustment anywhere in this post, and nothing to anchor the two schedules against each other.

## What Mississippi State does well

A 49.5% success rate, 6.96 yards a play, and 9.1% of snaps gaining 20 or more. But the number that separates them is what happens once they get close.

**Mississippi State scores a touchdown on 45% of its possessions.** LSU does on 36%. On trips inside the opponent's 40 the gap widens: **5.70 points per trip against 4.75**, with 19 touchdowns on 27 trips.

They also stay on the field. Six three-and-outs in 49 drives, against LSU's ten in fifty.

## What Mississippi State does badly

The defense does not get opponents off schedule.

  src="/static/images/mississippi-state-vs-lsu-downtype-defense.png"
  alt="Grouped bar chart of success rate allowed by down type. On standard downs Mississippi State allows 48 percent and LSU 38 percent. On passing downs Mississippi State allows 28 percent and LSU 24 percent."
  width={1380}
  height={873}
/>

On standard downs — first down, second-and-7 or less, third-and-4 or less — Mississippi State allows a **47.8% success rate**. LSU allows 37.7%. Once an offense is behind schedule the Bulldogs are respectable, at 27.7% allowed. The problem is that opponents rarely get there.

Look at the play counts under that chart. Mississippi State's defense has faced **205 standard downs and 83 passing downs**; LSU's has faced 154 and 90. That ratio is the whole story. Mississippi State's opponents spend their afternoons on first-and-10 and second-and-4, because nothing makes them stop.

The front is the reason: a 14.7% stuff rate, 6.4% sack rate and 8.7% hurry rate. Against Alabama they did not stop a single run at or behind the line in twenty-nine attempts.

## What LSU does well

Everything on defense, and it starts up front: **26.5% stuff rate, 9.6% sack rate, 14.4% hurry rate.** They allow 3.72 yards a carry and **4.66 yards per pass attempt**.

Then the part that should worry Mississippi State most. On passing downs LSU allows a **24.4% success rate and −0.502 expected points per play.** They knock you off schedule and then take the down away from you entirely.

Mississippi State's offense is excellent on standard downs (54.6% success) and ordinary behind schedule (38.0%). LSU's defense is built to make that distinction decide the game.

## What LSU does badly

The offense is the ninth-best in the conference and looks it: 6.22 yards a play, and only **6.5% of snaps gaining 20+** against Mississippi State's 9.1%. Ten three-and-outs in fifty drives. They turn it over on 20% of possessions.

Their lead back, D. Jones, has 86 carries at 4.41 a carry with a **38% opportunity rate** — the share of runs gaining at least four yards. He is the feast-or-famine profile I [wrote about two weeks ago](/blog/running-backs-and-the-average), and this season the famine is winning.

As passers they are nearly the same player: a point of completion percentage apart, six tenths of a yard per attempt apart, throwing to roughly the same depth. Everything that separates them happens at the end of the throw. **Taylor has sixteen touchdowns against four interceptions. Leavitt has five against seven.**

Leavitt earns it back on the ground, and more than people credit. **Seven rushing touchdowns to Taylor's one**, 6.2 yards a carry, and a 67% opportunity rate as a runner — better than his own lead back by a distance. Both of these teams have a quarterback as their most efficient runner, which is worth saying plainly, but only one of them is scoring that way.

## The part no number can price

Taylor was listed **questionable** on Wednesday's SEC availability report with a foot injury traced to the Missouri game, and he has been limited in practice since Alabama. **Fluff Bothwell is questionable too.** Jeff Lebby said Monday the focus is on getting Taylor ready to play.

If Taylor does not go, the backup who has taken meaningful snaps this season is P. Puckett: **14 attempts, 8 completions, 89 yards, no touchdowns, no interceptions**, all of it in mop-up time against UL Monroe and Alabama. Reporting on who would actually start is not consistent, so I will not guess.

Everything above describes a Mississippi State offense that is third in the SEC. That offense has been Kamario Taylor and Fluff Bothwell for five games. Against the best defense in the conference, in Tiger Stadium, a lineup without them is a different team than the one these numbers measure — and the tables stop being useful at exactly that point.

## What I would watch

Three things that should tell you early which way this is going:

- **Does Mississippi State stay on schedule?** Their offense is a 54.6% success rate on standard downs and 38.0% behind schedule. LSU's defense is built to force the second number.
- **Does LSU's front win first down?** A 26.5% stuff rate against a Bulldogs run game that has fallen every week — 9.72 yards a carry in week one down to 2.45 against Alabama.
- **Who starts at quarterback**, and if it is not Taylor, whether Mississippi State still plays like a team that scores on 45% of its drives.

---

_How these numbers are built: 2026 scrimmage plays, FBS opponents both ways, garbage time removed, kneels and spikes excluded. Success rate: a play succeeds if it gains 50% of the needed yards on first down, 70% on second, or 100% on third or fourth; turnovers always fail. Standard downs are first down, second-and-7 or less, and third or fourth and 4 or less. EPA is filtered to plays whose underlying expected-points values survive checking. Passer rating is the NCAA formula, computed from play-by-play yardage, which runs slightly short of official box scores because this data is missing plays in most 2026 games. Quarterback and ball-carrier names are parsed from the play description. Opportunity rate is the share of carries gaining four or more yards, following Bill Connelly. **Neither team's numbers are adjusted for opponent, and the two have played no common FBS opponent**, so the comparison rests entirely on unadjusted rates across five games each. Data from cfbfastR. Injury reporting as of Wednesday's SEC availability report._
