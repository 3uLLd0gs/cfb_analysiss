Mississippi State's most explosive offense in the last six seasons was not Mike Leach's Air Raid, and it was not last year's team either.

It was 2023. A team that went 5-7, scored the fewest points of any MSU offense in the period, and posted −0.135 expected points added per play — comfortably the worst mark on this list.

That offense also generated more yardage per successful play than Leach's 2021 team, which was the best MSU offense of the six.

Both of those things are true, and holding them at the same time requires a third metric.

## The thing EPA and success rate cannot tell you

Success rate answers how often an offense stays on schedule. EPA per play answers what the average snap was worth. Neither answers a question that matters just as much: **when this offense works, how much does it get?**

Two offenses can arrive at similar averages by opposite routes. One converts constantly and gains modest chunks. The other stalls repeatedly and then breaks something long. The season totals can land close together while the teams look nothing alike on any given Saturday.

## Isolating the explosiveness

The metric that separates them is IsoPPP, and the idea behind it is small enough to state in one line.

**Take only the successful plays. Average their EPA.**

That is the whole thing. Filter to the plays that worked, then ask how much they were worth.

The filter is what makes it useful. If you averaged EPA across _all_ plays you would just be measuring efficiency again — an offense with more successes would score higher whether or not those successes were big. Throwing away the failures holds consistency constant and isolates magnitude. Two offenses with very different success rates become comparable on the single question of what a working play returns.

A high IsoPPP offense gets a lot out of its successes. A low one gets first downs and not much else.

## Six years of Mississippi State

Same filters throughout: FBS opponents only, garbage time excluded.

| Season | Coach  | Success rate | IsoPPP    | EPA/play  |
| ------ | ------ | ------------ | --------- | --------- |
| 2020   | Leach  | 39.4%        | 1.197     | −0.154    |
| 2021   | Leach  | **49.5%**    | **1.101** | **0.108** |
| 2022   | Leach  | 44.9%        | 1.132     | −0.031    |
| 2023   | Arnett | 35.2%        | 1.341     | −0.135    |
| 2024   | Lebby  | 38.5%        | **1.468** | 0.009     |
| 2025   | Lebby  | 40.7%        | 1.332     | 0.022     |

Read the IsoPPP column against the EPA column and the relationship is close to inverted. The three most explosive seasons — 2024, 2023, 2025 — include the worst offense of the six. The least explosive season, 2021, is the best by a wide margin.

Leach's 2021 offense succeeded on nearly half its plays and rarely broke anything long. It moved by accumulation: short throws, on schedule, relentlessly. That is what 49.5% and 1.101 look like together.

Arnett's 2023 offense is the mirror image. It failed on two of every three snaps and then occasionally hit something big. The explosive plays were real, and they were nowhere near enough to offset the rest.

**Explosiveness is not a synonym for quality.** It is a description of shape.

## The two traits pull against each other

Across all of FBS in 2025, the correlation between success rate and IsoPPP is **−0.317** across 136 teams.

Negative. Weakly, but consistently: the more efficient an offense is, the less explosive it tends to be.

This is less strange than it sounds. An offense taking what the defense concedes — quick throws, runs into favorable boxes — converts often and rarely breaks anything long. An offense pushing the ball downfield fails more and returns more when it connects. Those are choices about risk, and you generally cannot have both.

Mississippi State, Texas, and Alabama are the cluster in the middle of the upper-left quadrant, close enough that the labels have to point at them. Three offenses within a point of each other on success rate, within 0.006 on IsoPPP, and all three sitting near zero EPA. Whatever separated Alabama's season from MSU's, it was not the shape of the offense.

One thing the chart does _not_ show is the tradeoff from the section above. There is no downward tilt to this cloud — across these 16 teams the correlation between success rate and IsoPPP is 0.004, which is nothing at all. That is what a −0.317 relationship looks like when you zoom into 16 of 136 teams: weak enough across the whole population that any one conference can lose it entirely. The tradeoff is a tendency in the aggregate, not a rule that binds each team.

What the color does show is that the two axes are not equally informative. Almost every red dot sits to the right of the vertical line; Texas is the only left-half offense in the conference's top half by EPA, and everything else over there is white or blue. Move up and down instead and the pattern disappears — Vanderbilt is red at the top of the chart, Tennessee and Georgia are red at the bottom, and the two darkest blues on the page, South Carolina and LSU, are sitting up in the explosive half.

A few pairs in there are worth pulling out of the cloud.

Ole Miss and Georgia are the cleanest pair. Nearly identical success rates — 47.1% and 46.4% — and a real gap in explosiveness, 1.242 against 1.138. Ole Miss ends up slightly ahead on EPA because its successful plays returned more. Same consistency, different payoff per success.

Then look at the two ends of the IsoPPP column. Arkansas is the most explosive offense in the conference at 0.155 EPA per play. South Carolina is sixth-most explosive at −0.094. Explosiveness alone tells you almost nothing about whether an offense was any good.

Vanderbilt is the anomaly: 50.8% and 1.381, both near the top, and 0.289 EPA per play — far clear of the field. Hold that thought. I will come back to Vanderbilt, and the answer involves who they played.

## The caveat that matters most

Here is the part I would rather not have found.

| Season pair | IsoPPP | Success rate | EPA/play |
| ----------- | ------ | ------------ | -------- |
| 2020 → 2021 | 0.330  | 0.409        | 0.344    |
| 2021 → 2022 | 0.193  | 0.534        | 0.478    |
| 2022 → 2023 | 0.409  | 0.443        | 0.405    |
| 2023 → 2024 | 0.399  | 0.377        | 0.320    |
| 2024 → 2025 | 0.352  | 0.386        | 0.339    |

These are year-over-year correlations: how well one season's number predicts the next for the same team.

**Success rate is the stickiest of the three**, averaging around 0.43. **IsoPPP is the least sticky**, averaging around 0.34, and one pair came in at 0.193 — close to no carryover at all.

The gap is smaller than I expected. I had assumed explosiveness would prove to be mostly noise while efficiency was a real team trait. The truth is milder: all three are only weakly predictive year to year, and IsoPPP is somewhat worse than the other two rather than categorically different.

That is still enough to change how I use it. Some of what IsoPPP measures is scheme and personnel, and some of it is a handful of long plays that happened to break — variance wearing a costume. So I treat it as descriptive rather than predictive. It is a good tool for saying _this is what that offense was_. It is a weaker tool for saying _this is what that offense will be_.

MSU's own history makes the point. From 1.341 in 2023 to 1.468 in 2024 to 1.332 in 2025, with three different results attached. Explosiveness moved around a lot for a program in the middle of a rebuild.

## Where this leaves the toolkit

Three numbers now, and each answers something the others cannot:

- **Success rate** — how often does this offense stay on schedule?
- **IsoPPP** — when it works, how much does it get?
- **EPA per play** — what is a snap worth, on average, accounting for both?

Together they describe an offense in a way yardage never could.

What they still do not do is account for who was on the other side of the ball. Every number in that SEC table treats a snap against Georgia and a snap against a Sun Belt opponent as the same event. They are not, and until that is fixed, every ranking on this site should be read with a hand over one eye.
