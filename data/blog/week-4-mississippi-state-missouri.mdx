Mississippi State beat Missouri 31-24 to go 4-0. It led 14-0, trailed 24-17, and scored the last 14 points.

Five days before the game I published [a scouting piece on Missouri](/blog/scouting-missouri). Almost every specific claim in it failed. That is where this has to start, because a preview nobody checks afterwards is just entertainment.

## What the scouting report said, and what happened

| Claim                                 | Through three weeks | Against each other        |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------- | ------------------------- |
| Mississippi State offense             | 56% success         | **45%**                   |
| Mississippi State on third-and-7+     | **0 of 19** allowed | 4 of 10 allowed           |
| Mississippi State on passing downs    | 16% allowed         | 38% allowed               |
| Missouri more explosive when it works | 1.51 vs 1.31        | 1.19 vs 1.16 — even       |
| Mississippi State runs left           | 10.2 yards a carry  | **4 carries, 0.75 yards** |
| Missouri offense                      | 35% success         | **43%**                   |

Per play, this was a coin flip. Mississippi State finished at +0.04 expected points added per play, Missouri at +0.06. The team that looked twenty points of success rate better on paper was not better at all for sixty minutes.

There are two honest readings. The first is that two Missouri games against Kansas and Troy were never enough to describe a team, which the piece said at the time and which is the reason it carried no prediction. The second is more uncomfortable: Missouri is better than its first three weeks suggested, and Mississippi State's early numbers were flattered by UL Monroe.

## The four numbers this blog keeps coming back to

Before the detail, the summary. These are the measures introduced across the first few posts here, applied to one game:

|                                                       | Mississippi State | Missouri   |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ----------------- | ---------- |
| **Success rate** — how often a play stays on schedule | **45%**           | 43%        |
| **EPA per play** — points added per snap              | +0.04             | **+0.06**  |
| **Explosiveness (IsoPPP)** — size of the successes    | 1.16              | **1.19**   |
| **Yards per play**                                    | **5.8**           | 5.6        |
| Plays of 20+ yards                                    | 4 (5% of snaps)   | **6 (8%)** |

Every one of those is a tie or a Missouri edge. Mississippi State won by seven anyway, and the rest of this post is about where the seven points came from — because it was not from being better per snap.

## Leading 14-0 while being outplayed

Mississippi State's first touchdown took one play: a 43-yard throw to S. Magee on the opening snap of the drive. Its second took eight. Between and after those two drives, the offense did almost nothing.

<Image
  src="/static/images/mississippi-state-vs-missouri-half-epa.png"
  alt="Bar chart of EPA per play by half. Mississippi State is at minus 0.25 in the first half and plus 0.32 in the second. Missouri is at plus 0.03 and plus 0.10."
  width={1260}
  height={820}
/>

**Minus 0.25 EPA per play is Mississippi State's worst half of the season** — worse than any half against South Carolina, Minnesota or UL Monroe. It happened while the scoreboard read 14-0.

That is the cleanest illustration of something this blog has argued from the first post: the scoreboard is a lagging, lumpy summary of play quality. Two plays produced fourteen points; thirty-five other snaps produced very little.

Missouri then scored on four of its next five possessions across the second and third quarters — field goal, touchdown, punt, touchdown, touchdown — to lead 24-17. Those four scoring drives needed 5, 4, 7 and 5 plays. Nothing about them was grinding.

## The second half was genuinely different

  src="/static/images/mississippi-state-vs-missouri-half-success.png"

|                         | 1st half | 2nd half  |
| ----------------------- | -------- | --------- |
| **MSU success rate**    | 38%      | **51%**   |
| **MSU EPA per play**    | −0.25    | **+0.32** |
| MSU yards per play      | 6.3      | 5.4       |
| Missouri success rate   | 44%      | 41%       |
| Missouri yards per play | 4.9      | **6.5**   |

Note the third row. Mississippi State's yards per play went _down_ while its success rate and EPA went up — it stopped living on two explosive plays and started stringing together ordinary ones. That is what a team looks like when it starts staying on schedule.

Third downs are where it showed up most.

<Image
  src="/static/images/mississippi-state-vs-missouri-half-third.png"
  alt="Bar chart of third down conversion rate by half. Mississippi State rises from 20 percent in the first half to 60 percent in the second. Missouri falls from 44 percent to 43 percent."
  width={1260}
  height={820}
/>

**1 of 5 became 6 of 10.** The two drives that won the game were 14 plays and 5 plays; the first of them converted two third downs and a fourth-and-1.

This is the reverse of the South Carolina game two weeks ago, where Mississippi State's efficiency barely moved between halves and what changed was how often it had the ball. Here the possessions were even — 13 drives to 12 — and the offense itself improved.

## On schedule, and off it

Split every snap by whether the offense was still on schedule. Standard downs are first down, second-and-7 or less, third or fourth and 4 or less; passing downs are everything else.

| Success rate      | Standard downs | Passing downs |
| ----------------- | -------------- | ------------- |
| Mississippi State | 49%            | 38%           |
| Missouri          | 45%            | 38%           |

Identical on passing downs, and that is the number worth sitting with. Mississippi State went into this game having allowed a **16%** success rate on passing downs through three games. Missouri posted 38%. The best defensive number in the scouting report did not survive contact with a fourth opponent — which is roughly what you would expect from a 49-snap sample, and a reason to treat all of these four-game figures as provisional.

## The trenches

|                                    | Mississippi State | Missouri |
| ---------------------------------- | ----------------- | -------- |

| Rushing success rate               | **44%**           | 42%      |

Mississippi State ran better on average and was stopped cold far more often — a quarter of its carries gained nothing. F. Bothwell carried 25 times for 122 yards, which is a good day built partly on a handful of runs rather than steady gains.

The defensive side is where Mississippi State clearly won:

| Defensive disruption                                  | Mississippi State | Missouri |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ----------------- | -------- |
| **Havoc rate** (TFLs, sacks, breakups, interceptions) | **26.7%**         | 14.1%    |
| Passes broken up                                      | **15**            | 4        |
| Sacks                                                 | 2                 | 4        |
| Runs stopped behind the line                          | 3                 | 2        |

**Missouri threw 17 incompletions, and the play-by-play credits a Mississippi State defender with breaking up 15 of them.** Mississippi State's own incompletions were broken up 4 times out of 10. Simmons was not missing; he was being contested.

## Two ways to throw for 300 yards

Both quarterbacks had strong days by the box score, and arrived there by opposite routes.

|                                    | K. Taylor (MSU)       | A. Simmons (Missouri) |
| ---------------------------------- | --------------------- | --------------------- |
| Line                               | 27 of 38, 3 TD, 1 INT | 23 of 39, 3 TD, 0 INT |
| **Passer rating**                  | **171.4**             | 147.9                 |
| **Average depth of target**        | 7.8                   | **11.3**              |
| **Air yards per attempt**          | 7.6                   | 11.3                  |
| **Share of yards after the catch** | **46%**               | 37%                   |
| **Sack rate taken**                | 9.5%                  | 4.9%                  |

Air yards are new to this blog — the distance the ball travels past the line of scrimmage, separated from what the receiver does afterwards. There is no air-yards column in the data; it is derived from the catch spot recorded in each play's description.

Missouri threw three and a half yards deeper per attempt. Mississippi State threw shorter and let receivers run, and paid for the shorter drops with double the sack rate.

Completion rate by how far the ball actually travelled:

| Depth           | Mississippi State | Missouri    |
| --------------- | ----------------- | ----------- |
| Behind the line | 8 of 10           | 3 of 4      |
| 0-4 yards       | 6 of 7            | 6 of 10     |
| 5-9 yards       | 4 of 7            | 6 of 6      |
| 10-19 yards     | 7 of 8            | **6 of 12** |
| 20+ yards       | 2 of 5            | 2 of 8      |

Missouri threw 20 balls at least 10 yards downfield and completed 8. Mississippi State threw 13 and completed 9. The intermediate range is where those 15 pass breakups lived.

Two players finished 21 yards apart and did nothing alike:

- **S. Magee** — 3 catches, 76 yards, average target depth **20.8 yards**, 2 yards after the catch. Two of the three were touchdowns, including the game-winner.
- **F. Bothwell** — 6 catches, 55 yards, average target depth **0.6 yards**, 48 yards after the catch. Plus 25 carries for 122.

**A. Evans III** was the volume target: 11 of the 38 attempts, 8 catches, 66 yards, split about evenly between air and run.

One offense, three different jobs — a deep threat who is thrown to twice a quarter, a back who catches at the line and runs, and a possession receiver who does both. That split is invisible in a box score that lists only catches and yards.

## Where the seven points came from

If the per-play numbers were even, something else decided it. Drives:

|                                    | Mississippi State | Missouri   |
| ---------------------------------- | ----------------- | ---------- |
| Drives                             | 13                | 12         |
| Average start                      | own 26            | **own 35** |
| Share of available yards gained    | 47%               | **54%**    |
| **Trips inside the opponent's 40** | 5                 | **8**      |
| **Touchdowns on those trips**      | **3 of 5**        | 3 of 8     |
| Three-and-outs                     | 2                 | 1          |

Missouri started nine yards closer to the end zone on average, gained a larger share of the field in front of it, and reached the Mississippi State 40 **eight times to Mississippi State's five**. It came away with three touchdowns. Mississippi State reached Missouri's 40 five times and came away with three, plus a 43-yard touchdown on a drive that never needed to get there.

Missouri's eight trips produced 24 points. Mississippi State's five produced 24, and the one-play touchdown made it 31.

That is the game. Not efficiency, not explosiveness, not field position — finishing, and a fourth-down stop at the 11 with 1:18 left.

## Four games in

| Opponent       | Success rate | EPA per play | Explosiveness |
| -------------- | ------------ | ------------ | ------------- |
| UL Monroe      | 60%          | +0.68        | 1.30          |
| Minnesota      | 62%          | +0.38        | 1.08          |
| South Carolina | 47%          | +0.37        | 1.59          |
| Missouri       | **45%**      | **+0.04**    | 1.16          |

Mississippi State is 4-0 and its per-play production has fallen in three consecutive weeks. Some of that is opponent quality climbing from a Sun Belt team to three power-conference defenses. Some of it is that the first number was never real.

The explosiveness column is the one to watch. It has not declined — the South Carolina game was the most explosive of the four. What has gone is the consistency underneath it: a 60% success rate in week one, 45% now. This is an offense that increasingly needs big plays because the ordinary ones have stopped coming, and it is 4-0 partly because the big plays keep arriving on time.

---

_How these numbers are built: scrimmage plays only, kneels and spikes removed. Neither team reached garbage time in this game, so all 153 snaps count. Success rate: a play succeeds if it gains 50% of the needed yards on first down, 70% on second, or 100% on third or fourth; turnovers always fail. Explosiveness (IsoPPP) is average EPA on successful plays only. Standard downs are first down, second-and-7 or less, and third or fourth and 4 or less. Passer rating is the NCAA formula. Air yards and yards after catch are derived from the catch spot in each play's description, covering about 85% of 2026 pass plays and almost none before 2025. Havoc counts tackles for loss, sacks, pass breakups, interceptions and forced fumbles as a share of snaps._

_Taylor's passer rating uses his official yardage, for the reason that follows. The play-by-play for this game is **missing a play**: on one second-half drive the ball moves 48 yards with nothing recorded, which is exactly why passing yards derived here total 312 against an official 360. Completions, attempts, touchdowns and interceptions all match ESPN; the yardage does not, and the air-yards totals exclude that play. Unexplained field-position jumps like it appear in nearly every 2026 game in this data. Data from cfbfastR._
