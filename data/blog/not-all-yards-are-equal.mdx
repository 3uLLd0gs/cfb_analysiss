Fluff Bothwell ran for one yard twice against Northern Illinois last season.

The first came in the first quarter, 4th and 1 near midfield. He got the yard, MSU kept the ball, and the drive continued.

The second came in the third quarter, 3rd and 2. He got the yard, and MSU punted.

The box score records both as a one-yard rush. Same player, same game, same result on the stat sheet. But by expected points, the first was worth **+2.57** and the second **−1.49** — a swing of more than four points between two plays that look identical in every traditional accounting.

**Q1, 5:49 — 4th and 1. EPA +2.57.** [Watch the play](https://youtu.be/ASXq7Xt6Yps?t=983)

**Q3, 7:50 — 3rd and 2. EPA −1.49.** [Watch the play](https://youtu.be/ASXq7Xt6Yps?t=5652)

That gap is what this post is about.

## Yards are a unit that changes value

Every stat you see on a broadcast is built on yards. Total offense, rushing yards, yards per game, yards per play. All of them add up yards as if a yard is a yard.

It isn't.

Three yards on 3rd and 2 is a conversion. Three yards on 3rd and 8 is a punt. Three yards from your own 20 and three yards from the opponent's 5 are different events with different consequences. A team can out-gain an opponent by a hundred yards and lose, and the yardage column gives you no way to see why.

Here is every situation from the 2025 season where an offense gained exactly three yards, with at least 100 such plays:

| Down and distance | Plays | EPA       |
| ----------------- | ----- | --------- |
| 3rd and 3         | 302   | **+1.47** |
| 1st and 3         | 233   | +1.06     |
| 3rd and 2         | 440   | +0.83     |
| 2nd and 3         | 382   | +0.76     |
| 3rd and 1         | 504   | +0.49     |
| 2nd and 5         | 466   | −0.18     |
| 1st and 10        | 6,526 | −0.33     |
| 2nd and 1         | 265   | −0.34     |
| 1st and 15        | 125   | −0.36     |

Same three yards. A range of 1.8 points, depending entirely on when it happened.

## Expected points

The fix is to stop measuring the play and start measuring the situation it left you in.

Take every play in college football history and sort it by game state: down, distance, yard line, time remaining. For each state, ask a simple question — historically, what were the next points scored? Positive if the team with the ball scored them, negative if the other team did.

The answers are not theoretical. They are just what happened, averaged over decades.

First and ten at your own 20 is worth around half a point. First and goal at the 5 is worth around five. Third and 15 at your own 5 is slightly **negative**, because from that spot the other team is more likely to score next than you are.

That is the expected points model. **EPA — expected points added — is simply the difference between the state before the snap and the state after it.**

Bothwell's 4th-and-1 conversion moved MSU from a state worth about half a point to a state worth about three. That difference is the +2.57. His 3rd-and-2 run left them facing 4th and 1 with a punt coming, which is worth less than where they started. That is the −1.49.

One number, and it handles down, distance, and field position automatically. It is why EPA became the standard.

## Big plays are not automatically valuable either

The obvious objection is that this is a story about short-yardage situations. It isn't.

Against Georgia last year, MSU had two of their best plays of the season:

| Quarter | Situation  | Yards gained | EPA       |
| ------- | ---------- | ------------ | --------- |
| 3rd     | 4th and 6  | **6**        | **+5.17** |
| 4th     | 1st and 10 | **57**       | **+5.12** |

A six-yard completion worth as much as a fifty-seven-yard one. The six-yarder converted a fourth down — fail and the drive ends immediately. The 57-yarder was a great play, but it came on first down with the drive alive either way.

Fifteen-yard runs vary just as much. A 15-yard run on 4th and 7 against Tennessee was worth +4.21. A 15-yard run on 1st and 10 against Wake Forest was worth +0.42. Both would be called explosive. One was worth ten times the other.

## Success rate: the companion metric

EPA tells you how much a play was worth. It does not tell you how often an offense works.

Success rate does. A play is successful if it keeps the drive on schedule:

- **1st down:** gain at least 50% of the distance
- **2nd down:** at least 70%
- **3rd and 4th down:** all of it
- **Turnovers always fail**

The thresholds rise because your margin shrinks. On 1st and 10, gaining 5 leaves 2nd and 5, which is comfortable. On 2nd and 10, gaining 5 leaves 3rd and 5, which is not. On third down there is no partial credit — you convert or you give the ball away.

Averaged over a season, success rate tells you how consistently an offense operates. It is the most stable number in football analytics, which means it describes what a team **is** rather than what happened to them.

## Where the two disagree

Look back at the three-yard table. Second and 1, gain three yards: that is a first down, so success rate counts it. But the EPA is **−0.34**.

Both are right. You stayed on schedule, so by the definition of success rate, the play worked. But you were already in an excellent position — 2nd and 1 is nearly a free down — and three yards barely improved it while burning the down.

That is the useful thing about carrying both numbers. Success rate answers _how often does this offense work_. EPA answers _how much is it worth when it does_. Teams can be very different on those two axes while posting similar totals, and I will come back to that in a later post.

## What this says about Mississippi State

Running MSU's 2025 offense through both, against FBS opponents only and with garbage time excluded:

- **EPA per play: +0.022**
- **Success rate: 40.7%**

Roughly league average on both. Not the disaster the record might suggest, and not close to the top of the conference either.

Those two numbers are more honest than any yardage total, but they are still incomplete. They do not adjust for who MSU played, and in the SEC that matters enormously — a schedule with Georgia and Texas on it is not the same test as one without. They also do not separate the two things I mentioned above: how often the offense worked versus how big it was when it did.

Both of those are the next two posts.
