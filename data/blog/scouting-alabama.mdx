Alabama comes to Starkville on Saturday at 4-0, having won by 38, 28, 14 and 31. It is averaging 48 points a game. Mississippi State is also 4-0.

This is a description of what both teams have done through four games, not a forecast. Two caveats travel with everything below: these are four-game samples, and the schedules are not comparable — Alabama has played East Carolina, Kentucky, Florida State and South Carolina; Mississippi State has played UL Monroe, Minnesota, South Carolina and Missouri.

They do share one opponent, which is the closest thing to a controlled test available in September. That comes at the end.

## The offenses are near-twins

|                        | Alabama   | Mississippi State |
| ---------------------- | --------- | ----------------- |
| Success rate           | 50.5%     | **52.5%**         |
| Yards per play         | **7.5**   | 7.3               |
| EPA per play           | **+0.41** | +0.31             |
| Explosiveness (IsoPPP) | **1.40**  | 1.27              |
| Plays of 20+ yards     | **10.2%** | 9.7%              |
| Rushing success        | 52.3%     | **55.3%**         |
| Passing success        | 52.0%     | **53.0%**         |
| Standard downs         | 56.6%     | 56.3%             |
| **Passing downs**      | 33.3%     | **43.7%**         |

Alabama is slightly better on a per-play basis and meaningfully more explosive. Mississippi State is better once it falls behind schedule — a ten-point edge on passing downs, which is the difference between a stalled drive and a punt avoided.

They are also nearly identical at the drive level, which is unusual:

|                                 | Alabama | Mississippi State |
| ------------------------------- | ------- | ----------------- |
| Drives                          | 40      | 40                |
| Share of available yards gained | 60.3%   | 59.7%             |
| Trips inside the opponent's 40  | 25      | 24                |
| **Touchdowns on those trips**   | **17**  | **17**            |
| Touchdown drives in total       | **21**  | 19                |
| Three-and-outs                  | 6       | 5                 |
| Average drive start             | own 33  | own 27            |

Two offenses, forty drives each, seventeen touchdowns each on almost exactly the same number of scoring chances. Two differences hide inside that. Alabama starts about five yards closer to the end zone. And four of its touchdown drives never registered as a trip inside the 40 at all, against two of Mississippi State's, because they reached the end zone from outside it — the explosiveness edge showing up as drives that skip the red zone entirely.

## The defenses fail in opposite ways

This is the most interesting thing in the comparison.

|                           | Alabama   | Mississippi State |
| ------------------------- | --------- | ----------------- |
| Success rate allowed      | **35.2%** | 39.0%             |
| Yards per play allowed    | 5.59      | **4.73**          |
| EPA allowed               | **−0.13** | +0.00             |
| **Explosiveness allowed** | 1.57      | **1.20**          |
| Sack rate                 | **7.9%**  | 5.4%              |
| Stuff rate on runs        | **25.5%** | 18.1%             |
| Pass breakups             | 11.9%     | **16.3%**         |
| Havoc rate                | 17.6%     | **18.3%**         |

Alabama is harder to move the ball on. It stops a quarter of all runs at or behind the line and sacks the quarterback on 7.9% of dropbacks. But when an offense does beat it, it beats it big.

Mississippi State's defense is the mirror image: beaten more often, rarely beaten badly. It allows the fewest yards per play of the two and the smaller explosive plays, while giving up a higher rate of ordinary successes.

In plain terms — Alabama's defense concedes fewer, larger chunks; Mississippi State's concedes more, smaller ones.

## One of these teams barely huddles

<Image
  src="/static/images/sec-2026-tempo.png"
  alt="Horizontal bar chart of the share of snaps run with no huddle for all sixteen SEC offenses. Mississippi State is at the top near 90 percent while Alabama is near the bottom around 7 percent."
  width={1707}
  height={1299}
/>

|                       | No huddle | Shotgun  | Run rate |
| --------------------- | --------- | -------- | -------- |
| **Mississippi State** | **90%**   | **100%** | 48%      |
| **Alabama**           | **7%**    | 73%      | 51%      |

Mississippi State has taken every single snap this season out of the shotgun, and huddles on roughly one snap in ten. Alabama huddles on more than nine of every ten, and is not marked in the shotgun on 27% of its snaps. What it lines up in instead the play-by-play does not say.

That is a genuine stylistic gulf, and it is one of the few things about scheme this data can actually measure. Run-pass balance, by contrast, is almost identical: 51% run for Alabama, 48% for Mississippi State.

**Alabama distributes; Mississippi State specialises.** Alabama's top three targets all live at essentially the same depth — 10.6, 10.6 and 11.1 yards — three interchangeable options on the same route tree. Mississippi State's five main targets run from 1.2 yards (a running back catching at the line) to 16.4 (a receiver who is only thrown to downfield), with a 34-target volume man in between who does most of his damage after the catch.

One offense is hard to key on because everyone is the same. The other is hard to key on because everyone is different.

Alabama's third-down defense is the number that stands out: **27% allowed overall, and 14% on third-and-7 or longer.** Mississippi State's offense converts 52% of its third downs. But the chart splits that open: Alabama has allowed 44% on third-and-3 or less and 40% on third-and-4-to-6, and Mississippi State converts 82% and 70% in those same two buckets. The 14% only applies once the offense is in third-and-long — and that is the bucket both teams land in most. Mississippi State has faced third-and-7-or-longer on 19 of its 40 third downs, and Alabama's defense has forced it on 22 of the 41 it has defended. Which of those two tendencies wins is the question the chart poses and cannot answer.

## The series

Mississippi State has not played Alabama since 2023 — the teams did not meet in 2024 or 2025. In the seasons this database covers, the series reads:

| Year | Site           | Result        |
| ---- | -------------- | ------------- |
| 2020 | Tuscaloosa     | Alabama 41-0  |
| 2021 | **Starkville** | Alabama 49-9  |
| 2022 | Tuscaloosa     | Alabama 30-6  |
| 2023 | **Starkville** | Alabama 40-17 |

Four meetings, four Alabama wins, by an average of 32 points. The two in Starkville were 49-9 and 40-17. None of those rosters or coaching staffs are the ones playing on Saturday, which is exactly why this post spends its length on what both teams have done this season instead.

## What this data cannot tell you

Worth stating plainly, because these are the questions everyone asks about a game like this:

- **Routes.** Not recorded. Route concepts appear in the play-by-play about thirty times across seventy-four thousand plays, which is to say not at all.
- **Defensive fronts.** Whether Alabama plays a three-man or four-man front is nowhere in this data. Neither is personnel grouping.
- **Play action, motion, coverage.** None of it is recorded.

Those come from watching film or from charting services, not from play-by-play. What the play-by-play does support is everything above: efficiency, explosiveness, down-type splits, drive quality, air yards, run direction, tempo, and disruption.

---

_How these numbers are built: scrimmage plays only, kneels and spikes removed, FBS opponents only, garbage time removed. Success rate: a play succeeds if it gains 50% of the needed yards on first down, 70% on second, or 100% on third or fourth; turnovers always fail. Explosiveness (IsoPPP) is average EPA on successful plays only. Standard downs are first down, second-and-7 or less, and third or fourth and 4 or less. Available yards is total yards gained divided by total yards available. Passer rating is the NCAA formula, computed from derived yardage, so both quarterbacks' ratings run slightly low by the same mechanism. Average depth of target and yards after catch are derived from the catch spot recorded in each play's description, covering about 90% of 2026 pass plays. Havoc counts tackles for loss, sacks, pass breakups, interceptions and forced fumbles as a share of snaps. Derived passing yardage runs slightly short of official box scores because the play-by-play is missing plays in most 2026 games. Data from cfbfastR._
